Toyota Motor (7203 JP): guidance raised and buyback increased, while net income stays 7.2% down

Operating profit flips from a decline to a gain, but the lines below it pull net income the other way

Toyota Motor raised its full-year profit forecast and increased its share buyback authorisation on November 5, 2026.

Follow the headlines alone, and it looks like a set of results with momentum behind it.

The net income forecast, however, is still below the prior year even after the raise.

This post reads the gap between the strong headline and the actual figures, using Toyota's own results presentation.

About this series: each post takes one listed Japanese company and reads its disclosures, from earnings materials to annual securities reports on EDINET, the regulator's filing system (Japan's counterpart to SEC EDGAR), where most documents are published only in Japanese.

Summary

  • The full-year operating profit forecast rises from JPY 3,400bn to JPY 3,900bn, which puts it 3.6% above the prior-year actual.
  • The full-year net income forecast also rises, from JPY 3,250bn to JPY 3,570bn, but it stays 7.2% below the prior-year actual of JPY 3,848.0bn.
  • The reason the two move in opposite directions shows up in "other income", the line below operating profit.
  • The buyback authorisation grows by JPY 300bn, taking the total for the year to a ceiling of JPY 1,300bn.

1. What the JPY 500bn operating profit raise is based on

Toyota Motor (https://global.toyota/) is one of Japan's largest manufacturers, building and selling cars around the world.

On November 5, 2026 it reported results for the second quarter (April to September) of the fiscal year ending March 2027.

At the same time it lifted its full-year operating profit forecast from JPY 3,400bn, the figure given in August, to JPY 3,900bn: a raise of JPY 500bn.

Operating profit is the profit earned by the core business, mainly by selling cars.

The prior year (the fiscal year ended March 2026) came in at JPY 3,766.2bn.

The August forecast was about 10% below that level; the new forecast is 3.6% above it.

In other words, the revision switches a forecast decline into a forecast increase.

The company explains the raise in its results presentation with two points.

One is lower tariff-related costs. The other is growth in the business that continues after a car is sold, such as accessories and parts (the value chain).

The materials say the forecast was set after also factoring in higher raw material costs (a drag of about JPY 1,000bn) and the situation in the Middle East.

The exchange rate assumption is JPY 157 per dollar, moved to a stronger yen than the JPY 160 assumed in August.

The company's view of operating profit is summed up in a single sentence.

we expect year-on-year profit growth, and have revised our forecast upward.

--- Toyota Motor, "FY2027 Second Quarter Financial Results" (November 5, 2026)

The "profit" in that sentence means operating profit.

In the same part of the materials, the JPY 3,900bn operating profit forecast is shown as positive against the prior year.

The Japan Times also reported that the company had raised its profit forecast.

For both operating profit and net income, the raise itself is a fact.

2. Why operating profit rises while net income falls

Next, the net income forecast in the same materials.

Net income is what is left for shareholders after tax and other charges are paid.

The forecast was JPY 3,250bn in August and is JPY 3,570bn this time.

The raise is JPY 320bn.

The prior-year actual, though, was JPY 3,848.0bn, so the forecast is 7.2% below it.

The Japan Times (carrying AFP and Jiji copy) also writes that the new forecast still means a 7.2% fall in net income year on year.

Why operating profit and net income point in opposite directions can be checked in the forecast table in the materials.

Operating profit rises against the prior year.

Below operating profit, however, "other income" is forecast to fall from JPY 1,386.7bn in the prior year to JPY 1,060bn, a drop of 23.6%.

In the table in the materials, other income includes equity-method investment gains and losses (forecast JPY 600bn, prior year JPY 552.7bn).

Equity-method investment gains and losses rise against the prior year.

Even so, because other income as a whole falls, pre-tax income comes to JPY 4,960bn, which is 3.7% below the prior year's JPY 5,152.9bn.

Net income is what remains after tax and other items are deducted, so the decline widens to 7.2%.

Net income as a share of pre-tax income drops from 74.7% in the prior year to 72.0% in the forecast.

What sits inside other income, and why the tax and other charges increase, are not written in these materials.

What can be read from the materials goes only as far as this: even with operating profit rising, the fall in the parts outside operating profit is forecast to be larger.

3. What the April to September actuals imply for second-half net income

In the first half (April to September), net income was JPY 2,236.4bn.

That is 26.1% more than the JPY 1,773.4bn of the same period a year earlier.

First-half operating profit was JPY 2,191.1bn, so net income was larger than operating profit.

The reason is that first-half other income rose by JPY 290.8bn from a year earlier, to JPY 763.2bn.

Subtracting the first-half actual from the full-year forecast gives the figures placed in the second half (October to March).

This subtraction is the author's own calculation; the company does not disclose a second-half forecast directly.

  • Operating profit: JPY 3,900bn minus JPY 2,191.1bn leaves JPY 1,708.9bn, which is 2.9% less than the prior-year second half (JPY 1,760.6bn).
  • Other income: JPY 1,060bn minus JPY 763.2bn leaves JPY 296.8bn.
  • Net income: JPY 3,570bn minus JPY 2,236.4bn leaves JPY 1,333.6bn, which is 35.7% less than the prior-year second half (JPY 2,074.6bn).

Second-half operating profit is roughly flat against the prior year.

Second-half net income, by contrast, works out at roughly six-tenths of the prior year.

For the full year, net income looks like a 7.2% decline, but the first-half increase has softened the size of the fall.

Cut out the second half alone, and the drop is much larger.

Of course, this is a subtraction between the company's own forecast figures.

The company may be setting its forecast conservatively, and other income may come in higher than assumed in the second half.

Which of the two it will be cannot be judged from these materials.

4. What the larger buyback changes about how profit looks

The company also announced shareholder returns.

It raised its buyback authorisation by JPY 300bn, taking the total for the year to a ceiling of JPY 1,300bn.

The company explains that the reason was a request from shareholders who wanted to sell their shares.

At the same time, it will cancel a further 1bn of its own shares.

Cancellations for the year come to 1.2bn shares in total, worth about JPY 3,500bn at the market price as of the end of September 2026, the materials say.

A cancellation is a procedure that extinguishes shares the company holds; no cash leaves the company.

The company says it will lower its level of treasury shares and pursue a capital policy that emphasises capital efficiency.

The dividend is unchanged from the forecast at the start of the year: an interim dividend of JPY 50 per share (up JPY 5 year on year) and a full-year forecast of JPY 100 (also up JPY 5).

Now place the profit forecast and the size of the authorisation side by side.

The net income forecast was raised by JPY 320bn, almost the same size as the JPY 300bn increase in the buyback authorisation.

The JPY 1,300bn authorisation for the year equals 36% of the JPY 3,570bn net income forecast.

The authorisation is a ceiling, not an amount actually bought.

The prior year's authorisation of JPY 3,656.8bn included the tender offer connected with taking Toyota Industries private.

For that reason, the JPY 1,300bn for this year and last year's figure cannot be compared directly.

A buyback reduces the number of shares in circulation by the amount bought, which works in the direction of lifting earnings per share.

That effect, though, is a separate matter from the level of operating profit or net income itself.

The fact that net income is below the prior year does not change because the authorisation is larger.

5. How to read the strong headline against the numbers

Operating profit: the forecast moved from a decline to an increase.

The buyback authorisation grew, and the dividend forecast of JPY 100 per share is maintained.

Net income: the forecast was raised by JPY 320bn, but it remains 7.2% below the prior year.

Cut out the second half alone, and the net income forecast is roughly six-tenths of the prior year.

Neither figure is wrong.

Operating profit is what the core business earns; net income is what finally remains for shareholders. The two simply look at different places.

The net income forecast was raised as well, but it stays below the prior year, and only operating profit turns to growth.

These materials do not explain what sits inside the other income that creates the gap, so readers have to check that part carefully.

Watching how the forecast moves at the third-quarter results for the fiscal year ending March 2027 will show a little more clearly whether the second-half net income was set conservatively.

Why it matters for global investors

Toyota's fiscal year ends on March 31, so the year discussed here (the fiscal year ending March 2027) runs from April 2026 to March 2027, and the first half is April to September. A Japanese income statement carries a block of lines between operating profit and pre-tax income, and that block is where the operating profit and net income forecasts part company: here it is "other income", which includes equity-method investment gains and losses, and the presentation does not break it down further. Toyota publishes an English version of this presentation, but most Japanese companies publish their earnings materials and annual securities reports in Japanese only, on EDINET, Japan's FSA filing system (the counterpart to SEC EDGAR). Two further points are worth keeping in mind: a buyback authorisation is a ceiling rather than an amount actually bought, and cancelling treasury shares is a common part of Japanese capital policy.

Closing

A raised operating profit forecast and a larger buyback look like the same piece of good news in a headline.

Which profit figure is being talked about, and whether the authorisation is a ceiling or an actual purchase, change what can be read from it.

Next time you open a set of results, try reading the lines just below operating profit, down to net income.
