Japan Imported 'Maxxing' With Only One Wing: Why Adult Hobbies Feel Hard

The word for a hobby you neither monetize nor optimize never arrived, while the market kept narrowing

"Do you find it hard to have a hobby as an adult?"

It is a question I hear often. The usual answers are time and money. But is that really all of it?

This post looks at the question through a word that emerged in English in 2026, and through some hard numbers from Japan.

About this series: each post takes a listed Japanese company and reads its disclosures on EDINET, the Japanese regulator's filing system (Japan's counterpart to SEC EDGAR), where most documents are published only in Japanese. This edition starts from a question about culture and ends in one company's filings.

Summary

  • In Japanese, an adult hobby has only two ready-made landing spots: fandom spending (oshi-katsu) or a side business and self-improvement project. There is no common phrase that legitimizes a hobby you do not get better at, do not earn from, and do not broadcast.
  • In 2026 the English-speaking world named the opposite attitude "hobby-maxxing". The "maxxing" suffix itself has already been imported into Japanese, but the lists it appears in cover looks, sleep, fibre, protein and aura — self-optimization vocabulary. Hobby-maxxing is not among them.
  • The phenomenon itself, a return to analog hobbies, has been covered in Japan by Forbes JAPAN. What has not arrived is the word and the norm: it is fine to be bad at it, and fine not to earn from it.
  • In the Leisure White Paper, the average number of leisure activities per person has fallen for three years in a row, while the leisure market as a whole is larger than before the pandemic. Hobbies are narrowing into spending such as fandom, or investment such as side work.
  • As one retail example from hands-on hobbies, the post also looks at the filings of Japan Craft Holdings (7135), which runs craft stores. Mentioning a specific company is not a recommendation.

1. Search for "adult hobbies" and you get side work or fandom

Try searching for the Japanese phrase for "adult hobbies".

The top results are things like "10 side businesses that use your hobby", or first-person stories about starting an AI side business to pay for fandom goods.

By the time the search results appear, a hobby is already framed either as a way to earn or as something to worship. This is specific to the Japanese-language internet. It is a problem of vocabulary, separate from any individual's lack of time or money.

You do not have to improve. You do not have to earn. You do not have to publish.

Japanese has no phrase that says all of this outright, so when you try to start a hobby, you slide into one of the two molds.

2. In 2026, English named it "hobby-maxxing"

English has a word for the attitude: "hobby-maxxing".

It traces to TikTok creator @misshannahashton, and on May 7, 2026 Yahoo Lifestyle ran an explainer for a general audience titled "Hobby-Maxxing Explained: The 2026 Trend Replacing Doomscrolling With Real Life".

The norm behind the word is explicit. You do not have to make mastery or improvement the goal. You do not have to monetize it. In other words, the term gives a name to stepping off the optimization treadmill, and treats that as something you can claim openly.

The return to knitting, pottery and gardening is happening in the English-speaking world at the same time.

3. Why the imported "maxxing" has only one wing

What stands out is that "maxxing" itself has already been imported into Japanese.

NEW STANDARD Inc. (Setagaya, Tokyo; a company that runs media and brand support for millennials and Gen Z; website: https://new-standard.co.jp/) publishes NEW STANDARD, a media outlet for those generations. On December 20, 2025 it ran an explainer on the meaning of "maxxing" and how self-improvement among Gen Z is heading toward maximizing potential.

The five terms it lists are looksmaxxing, sleepmaxxing, fibremaxxing, proteinmaxxing and auramaxxing.

All five belong to self-improvement, which is to say self-optimization. Hobby-maxxing is not included.

This is not because the phenomenon itself never reached Japan.

Forbes JAPAN, a business media outlet, ran a piece on April 15, 2026 titled "In 2026, analog becomes a luxury: Gen Z goes offline", introducing the return to analog hobbies.

But that piece treats the phenomenon as a consumer-trend forecast. It does not go as far as naming the norm that it is fine to be bad, and fine not to earn.

So what happened in Japan is an asymmetry: the word "maxxing" arrived, but only the self-optimization wing grew. The wing for stepping off optimization was left behind.

4. Fewer activities, a bigger market

The numbers that support this asymmetry come from the Japan Productivity Center (a public-interest foundation founded in 1955 that researches and advises on labour productivity and has published the Leisure White Paper every year since 1977; website: https://www.jpc-net.jp/).

According to the preliminary edition of the Leisure White Paper 2026, released on July 16, 2026, the average number of leisure activities per person was 10.0.

That is down 0.2 from the year before: 10.4, then 10.2, then 10.0, three consecutive declines.

The share of people who say they value leisure also fell to 66.3%, from 67.8% the year before.

Meanwhile, according to the Leisure White Paper 2025 (covering 2024 results), the leisure-related market was worth JPY 75.2 trillion, above its pre-pandemic level.

The number of participants is falling in many activities, yet the market's value is growing. That looks contradictory.

What explains it is a skew in what the market contains.

According to Nikkei Cross Trend, a digital media outlet on marketing run by the Nikkei group (website: https://xtrend.nikkei.com/), the fandom market grew by close to a third in three years, to JPY 3.4 trillion.

The range of activities an individual takes up is shrinking, while spending on the few that remain is intense.

This is not an argument against fandom spending. It is one answer to "why are hobbies hard" that actually works.

The problem is the other landing spot. Taking up many things shallowly and enjoying them while staying bad is thinning out, both in the market and in the language.

5. The retail environment for hands-on hobbies

What kind of company sits inside this decline in the number of activities?

One example is Japan Craft Holdings (securities code 7135), which runs the craft store chain Craft Heart Tokai and operates craft-supply stores across Japan. It changed its name to the current one in October 2022. Website: https://www.jcraft-hd.co.jp/

The figures below are from its annual securities report filed on EDINET (document ID: S100WQJ2).

Revenue peaked at JPY 16.99bn in FY2023 and fell to JPY 14.05bn in FY2025, a 17% decline.

Net income was negative for four consecutive years.

In FY2025, however, operating profit turned positive at JPY 0.066bn.

That came from cutting selling, general and administrative expenses from JPY 9.90bn to JPY 8.00bn and reducing headcount from 277 to 247. Revenue itself did not grow.

Operating cash flow was minus JPY 0.50bn, so the improvement is still in progress.

The equity ratio also fell from 61.1% in FY2022 to 31.8% in FY2025.

At the same time as the English-speaking world was rediscovering knitting and crafts, Japanese craft retail was facing these numbers.

This loss cannot be taken as representative of the whole industry.

Company-specific factors also played a part, including the move to a holding-company structure, store impairments, and the reversal of a pandemic-era boom.

Treat it as one example of the environment in which hands-on hobby retail is operating.

Why it matters for global investors

Japan's disclosure is still overwhelmingly Japanese-only. The Leisure White Paper releases cited here, the Nikkei Cross Trend article and the annual securities report on EDINET are all Japanese-language documents, so the vocabulary gap described in this post is also an information gap for readers outside Japan. When a Japanese consumer trend is described abroad as "analog" or "slow living", the difference between spending-driven fandom and non-monetized hobby activity is rarely visible. That difference is worth keeping in mind when reading Japanese retail and consumer names.

Closing

Why does an adult hobby feel hard? The answer may not be a lack of time or money, but a lack of words.

Try saying it out loud once: "It is fine to stay bad at it. I do not have to earn from it."

That alone should change your stance a little the next time you start something.
